In statistics, probability is the mathematical measure of the likelihood that a specific event will occur. It quantifies uncertainty, allowing you to predict patterns in data, make informed decisions, and understand the risks associated with random events

Probabilities are always expressed as a number between 0 and 1 or 0% to 100%

Sample Space: The complete set of all possible outcomes in an experiment (e.g., for a coin flip, the sample space is {Heads, Tails}).
Event: A specific subset of the sample space you are measuring (e.g., the coin landing on Heads).
Independent Events: When the outcome of one event does not affect the probability of another (e.g., rolling a die twice)

In probability and statistics, an event is a specific outcome or a set of outcomes from a random experiment (a subset of the overall sample space). Events are broadly classified by how their outcomes occur, their likelihood, and their relationship to one another

1. Simple vs. Compound Events

Simple Event: Contains exactly one single outcome from the sample space. For example, rolling a die and getting exactly a \(4\).

Compound Event: Contains more than one single outcome. For example, rolling an even number on a die—getting a \(2\), \(4\), or \(6\).

2. Independent vs. Dependent Events

Independent Events: The occurrence of one event has absolutely no effect on the probability of the other. For example, flipping a coin and rolling a die, or drawing a card, replacing it, and drawing again.

Dependent Events: The occurrence of one event directly changes the probability of the other event. A classic example is drawing a card from a deck and drawing a second card without replacing the first one.

3. Mutually Exclusive vs. Exhaustive Events

Mutually Exclusive (Disjoint) Events: Two events that cannot occur at the same time. If one happens, the other cannot. For example, getting both a Heads and a Tails on a single coin flip.

Exhaustive Events: A set of events that together make up the entire possible set of outcomes. For instance, the events "rolling an even number" and "rolling an odd number" on a six-sided die cover every possible outcome.

4. Special Event Classifications Sure (Certain) Event: An event that is guaranteed to happen, with a probability of \(1\). For example, rolling a number less than \(7\) on a standard six-sided die.

Impossible Event: An event that can never occur, with a probability of \(0\). For example, rolling an \(8\) on a standard six-sided die.

Complementary Event: The exact opposite of an event occurring. If event \(A\) is rolling a \(3\) on a die, the complement (often written as \(A^{\prime }\) or \(A^{c}\)) is rolling anything other than a \(3\). The sum of probabilities for an event and its complement is always \(1\).

Equally Likely Events: Events that have the exact same chance of occurring. For example, on a fair die, rolling a \(1\), \(2\), \(3\), \(4\), \(5\), or \(6\) are all equally likely events
