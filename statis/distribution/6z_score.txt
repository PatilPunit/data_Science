In statistics, the empirical rule (or 68-95-99.7 rule) dictates how data is dispersed around the mean in a normal distribution (bell curve). It reveals how standard deviations—represented by z-scores—correspond to the percentage of total data.

A z-score measures exactly how many standard deviations a specific data point lies above or below the mean.

The empirical rule breaks this down as follows:

~68% of data falls within \(1\) standard deviation of the mean (Z-scores between \(-1\) and \(1\)).
~95% of data falls within \(2\) standard deviations of the mean (Z-scores between \(-2\) and \(2\)).
~99.7% of data falls within \(3\) standard deviations of the mean (Z-scores between \(-3\) and \(3\)).

Breaking Down the Z-Score Intervals

To visualize the spread of the curve, these percentages are distributed symmetrically:

Why It Matters 
Identifying Outliers: Data points with a z-score beyond \(\pm 3\) represent extreme outliers, as they make up only \(0.3\%\) of the total distribution.

A z-score (or standard score) tells you how many standard deviations a data point is from the mean of a dataset. 
It indicates whether a specific score is typical or unusual, and allows you to compare data points from entirely different datasets.

The Basics 

Positive z-score: The data point is above the mean.
Negative z-score: The data point is below the mean.
Zero z-score: The data point is exactly equal to the mean.

For example, if you take an exam and get a z-score of \(1.5\), your score is \(1.5\) standard deviations above the class average.

How to Calculate ItYou can calculate a z-score using the following formula:

\(z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\)\(z\) = Z-score\(x\) = Your data point or raw score\(\mu \) = The population mean (average)\(\sigma \) = The population standard deviation
